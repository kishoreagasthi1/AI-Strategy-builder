# VYNE™ — Interviewee Guide

**Audience:** a client executive who has been invited to a VYNE interview and has never seen it before.
**Length:** four to five minutes.
**Purpose:** to hand to NotebookLM (or a human narrator) together with the `shots/` folder, to produce the short video a consultant attaches to the invitation email.

---

## Notes for whoever produces this

This is deliberately short. It is watched by a busy person immediately before they do the thing it describes, so it answers only what they will actually wonder: what is this, how long, who sees it, what do I press, and what happens if I have to stop.

**Tone:** calm and plain. This person did not choose to be here — somebody in their organisation volunteered them. They are giving up forty minutes and they want to know it is not a trap.

**Two things to keep in, whatever else gets cut:**

1. Pause is safe. Submit is final.
2. They will never be shown a colleague's answers, and nobody will be shown theirs word for word without the consulting team's involvement.

**The screenshots** were captured from the running product using an invented company. The person in them, Peter Osei, is fictional.

### Scene 1 — What this is

**Screenshot:** `shots/03-20-interviewee-welcome.png`
**On screen:** An interview, with an AI interviewer

**Narration:**
You have been invited to take part in an AI readiness assessment of your organisation. This is the screen you will see when you sign in.

It is an interview, and the interviewer is an AI agent. It will ask you about your part of the business, follow up on what you say, and move on when it has what it needs. There is nothing to install, nothing to prepare, and nothing to configure — the invitation already knows who you are, what your role is, and which organisation this is about.

You will be in the chair for somewhere between twenty and ninety minutes. The consulting team set that length when they invited you, based on what they were told about your time — so if it does not match what you agreed, tell them rather than rushing. It is one sitting if you want it to be, and it does not have to be.

### Scene 2 — What will be covered

**Screenshot:** `shots/03-21-interviewee-topics.png`
**On screen:** Areas, not a script

**Narration:**
If you would like to know what is coming, you can preview the areas beforehand.

What you get is areas rather than an exact list of questions, and that is intentional in both directions. The interviewer adapts to what you say, so a fixed script would not survive the conversation anyway — and a script would tempt anyone into preparing answers, which is not what is useful here. Your unrehearsed view of how things actually work is the whole point.

The one thing worth doing is glancing at the areas and pulling up any rough figures you would rather not guess at.

### Scene 3 — The interview

**Screenshot:** `shots/03-22-interviewee-live.png`
**On screen:** Speak or type — both work

**Narration:**
Here is the interview itself.

The conversation runs down the middle. You can speak your answers, using the microphone, or click "Type Instead" and type them — both work equally well, and you can switch between them mid-interview. Some people find it easier to talk; some would rather write. Neither is treated differently.

On the right, a scorecard forms as you talk. You are welcome to watch it. It is not a test and there is no passing mark — it is a readiness picture across seven areas, and a low score simply means that area has not been built yet.

Your answers save as you go. There is no save button because there does not need to be one.

### Scene 4 — If you said something wrong

**Screenshot:** `shots/03-11-live-transcript.png`
**On screen:** "Clarify this answer"

**Narration:**
Under each of your own answers there is a "Clarify this answer" link.

Use it whenever you have thought better of something, remembered a figure, or realised the question meant something other than what you answered. Your clarification is folded into the assessment. It does not delete what you said first — both are kept — so there is no cost to correcting yourself and no need to get it right first time.

---

### Scene 5 — Stopping and coming back

**Screenshot:** `shots/03-23-interviewee-controls.png`
**On screen:** Pause is safe. Submit is final.

**Narration:**
Two buttons at the top, and the difference between them matters.

**Pause — continue later** is completely safe. Everything you have said is already saved. You can close the window, take a call, and come back tomorrow.

**Finish & Submit Interview** is the one that ends it. It sends your interview to the consulting team and nothing can be changed afterwards. Only press it when you are done.

If you are unsure which you want — you want Pause.

---

### Scene 6 — Coming back

**Screenshot:** `shots/03-24-interviewee-paused.png`
**On screen:** Continue where you left off

**Narration:**
This is what pausing looks like. You can close the window entirely.

When you sign in again you will be offered "Continue where you left off", and the conversation picks up exactly where it stopped, with everything you have already said intact. You do not repeat anything.

---

### Scene 7 — Finishing

**Screenshot:** `shots/03-25-interviewee-submitted.png`
**On screen:** Done — nothing further is needed from you

**Narration:**
And this is what it looks like when you have submitted. Your interview is with the consulting team and every control is switched off, so there is no ambiguity about whether it went through.

Nothing further is needed from you. If the consulting team wants a short follow-up conversation later — usually because two people described something differently and it is worth understanding why — you will receive a separate invitation for it.

---

### Scene 8 — What happens to what you said

**Screenshot:** `shots/03-22-interviewee-live.png`
**On screen:** Your view is one of several

**Narration:**
A word on where this goes, because it is the reasonable question.

Your answers are read by the consulting team running the assessment. Your view is combined with those of the other people being interviewed to produce a picture of the organisation — and where people see things differently, that difference is treated as useful information rather than as somebody being wrong. It is very often the most valuable thing to come out of the exercise.

You cannot see anyone else's answers here, and this screen has no route into anything else. It is your interview and nothing more.

The most useful thing you can do is answer as things actually are, including the parts that are held together with a spreadsheet and somebody's goodwill. That is precisely what an honest readiness assessment is trying to find, and nobody is scored on it personally.

Thank you for the time.
