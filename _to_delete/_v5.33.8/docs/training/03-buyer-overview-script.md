# VYNE™ — Platform Overview for Prospective Firms

**Audience:** a principal or partner at a consulting firm evaluating VYNE as a platform to run AI readiness engagements on.
**Length:** eight to ten minutes.
**Purpose:** to hand to NotebookLM (or a human narrator) together with the `shots/` folder, to produce the overview video that goes on the website and into a first-meeting follow-up.

---

## Notes for whoever produces this

The person watching this already runs engagements. They are not evaluating whether AI readiness assessments are worth doing — they are evaluating whether this makes theirs faster, more defensible, or more repeatable across a team.

**Three claims this video makes, in order.** Each one is shown on a real screen rather than asserted:

1. **The evidence is traceable.** Every number can be traced to who said it, live, in front of a client.
2. **The disagreements are the product.** Most tools average people together; this one keeps them apart and surfaces where they differ.
3. **It is a chain, not a folder of documents.** The briefing tunes the interviews, the interviews produce the synthesis, the synthesis produces the roadmap, the roadmap produces the design briefs.

**Do not overclaim.** The audience is sceptical by trade and will test anything that sounds like a promise. Where the product is opinionated — the three roadmap gates, the refusal to score an untouched dimension — say so plainly; those are the moments that land with this audience, because they read as a product that has been used rather than demoed.

**The screenshots** are captured from the running product against an invented client, Northwind Freight Group. Say so once, early. This audience will assume a mockup otherwise, and the fact that these are real screens is part of the argument.

### Scene 1 — The problem this is built around

**Screenshot:** `shots/00-03-hub.png`
**On screen:** One engagement, five stages, one chain

**Narration:**
An AI readiness engagement is not hard because the analysis is hard. It is hard because of what sits between the analysis and the deliverable.

Six or eight executive interviews, each conducted slightly differently by whoever was free that week. Notes in three formats. A scoring model that lives in a partner's head. A roadmap assembled in PowerPoint the weekend before the readout, where the link between what somebody said in week one and what is on the plan in week six exists only in the memory of the person who wrote both.

VYNE is that chain, built as software. Briefing, interviews, synthesis, roadmap, solution design. Each stage reads what the last one wrote — and everything you are about to see is the running product, working on an invented logistics client, not a mockup.

### Scene 2 — The briefing tunes everything downstream

**Screenshot:** `shots/01-70-briefing-full.png`
**On screen:** Hypotheses, issue tree, stakeholder map — before anyone is interviewed

**Narration:**
An engagement starts with a briefing, and the briefing is not a document — it is configuration.

The industry and revenue band select the benchmark set the client is scored against. The hypotheses are the starting positions the interviews will test. The issue tree is the coverage check. The stakeholder map, at the bottom, sets not just who is interviewed but how deeply each role is questioned on each of the seven dimensions — because a CFO does not need five questions on data lineage.

That last list drives every interview the platform runs. Which means the thing that most determines the quality of a VYNE engagement is a piece of work your firm already does before every engagement anyway. It is just written down somewhere it can be executed.

### Scene 3 — Interviews that are consistent by construction

**Screenshot:** `shots/03-22-interviewee-live.png`
**On screen:** The same interview, however many people run it

**Narration:**
This is a client executive being interviewed. The interviewer is an AI agent, working to the briefing, following up on what it hears rather than reading a script. They can speak or type.

The commercial point is not that it saves a consultant's hour, though it does. It is consistency. Six executives get the same rigour whether they were interviewed in week one or week three, by your most experienced partner or by somebody in their second month. The depth setting — twenty-five, thirty-five or fifty questions — is chosen deliberately per person rather than emerging from how much time whoever ran it happened to have.

And their view of the platform is exactly this: one interview, no route into anything else. That is the answer to the client security question before it is asked.

### Scene 4 — The scoring is transparent

**Screenshot:** `shots/04-12-drill-dimension.png`
**On screen:** "Why is that a 2.4?" — answered from the product

**Narration:**
This is the moment that tends to decide the evaluation.

A client asks why a dimension scored what it scored. You click it, and the platform shows the arithmetic: every interview, its score, the weight applied to that role on that dimension, and the sum. Live, in the meeting.

Weighting matters here. A CTO's reading of the technology estate counts for more than the CFO's, and the reverse is true on the business case — so the number is not a flat average of whoever happened to be available. And a dimension nobody was asked about stays "not yet assessed" rather than quietly becoming a zero.

The comparison is not with a better spreadsheet. It is with the alternative, which is a partner saying "in our judgement, this is a two point four."

### Scene 5 — Disagreement is treated as evidence

**Screenshot:** `shots/04-13-drill-contradiction.png`
**On screen:** Two regional operations leaders. Same process. Three points apart.

**Narration:**
This is the part of the product that consultants tend to react to.

Here are two people at the same company, holding the same job title, three points apart on the same process. The platform did not average them into a single "operations" score. It kept them as separate voices and then put the gap on the front page, with what each of them actually said.

That gap is usually the engagement. The distance between what leadership believes is happening and what the people running the operation experience is the finding a client will pay to have surfaced — and it is precisely what disappears the moment two interviews with the same job title get merged.

Two people with one title is not an edge case. It is any organisation with regions, divisions or business units.

### Scene 6 — The whole picture, derived rather than typed

**Screenshot:** `shots/04-02-synthesis-loaded.png`
**On screen:** Five interviews, one page, nothing typed by a consultant

**Narration:**
Five interviews on one page. Progress and coverage at the top, the scores with their evidence in the middle, what to do about it at the bottom.

Three panels are worth pointing out. **Confirmed findings** are the things more than one person raised independently — corroboration is the test, so the CEO and the CTO describing the same problem in different words is worth more than either saying it twice. **Contradictions** we have just seen. **Unresolved gaps** lists, per dimension, the roles with real authority that nobody has interviewed yet — which is your confidence gap stated as a list of people to go and get.

Nothing on this page was typed by a consultant. That is a different conversation to have with a client than presenting a slide.

### Scene 7 — A written argument, that a consultant signs

**Screenshot:** `shots/04-16-narrative-editor.png`
**On screen:** The model drafts. The consultant signs.

**Narration:**
The platform writes the synthesis — which hypotheses survived, what the client cannot see about itself, what follows.

And then a consultant edits it, and the edit is what goes to the client, marked as edited by a consultant.

That is the governance position of the whole product, and it is worth being explicit about it in a market where firms are being asked hard questions about AI in client work. The model drafts. The consultant signs. Nothing goes out under your firm's name that a person did not read and approve.

### Scene 8 — The plan is computed, and it argues back

**Screenshot:** `shots/05-19-roadmap-synthesis.png`
**On screen:** One investment unlocks four initiatives

**Narration:**
The scores go to the roadmap builder, which is where they become a sequenced plan.

Look at the shared investments table. One automated reconciliation unlocks four separate initiatives. That is the sequencing argument, and it is the difference between a phased plan and a wish list with dates on it — because "we should do this first, and here are the four things that then become possible" is a sentence that gets a phase funded.

Underneath it: the critical path, the phases and what each unlocks, the delivery constraints, and an executive narrative. And a thirty-six month Gantt you can drag in front of a client, where confirmed dependencies re-enforce themselves after a drag so the sequencing cannot be quietly broken by an enthusiastic sponsor in the room.

### Scene 9 — It refuses to produce a plan it cannot stand behind

**Screenshot:** `shots/05-17-gate-3-stages.png`
**On screen:** Three gates: selection, requirements, durations

**Narration:**
This screen is here on purpose, because it is the one that tells you what kind of product this is.

The roadmap tab will not open until three conditions are met. Something has to be selected. Every selected use case has to have a real, industry-calibrated capability profile rather than a generic default — otherwise its readiness percentage is not trustworthy. And every one has to carry a duration somebody confirmed.

The platform would rather show you a locked tab and tell you which of the three you are missing than produce a confident-looking roadmap resting on defaults. Every consultant watching this has been handed a plan built on numbers nobody stood behind. This is the product refusing to be that.

### Scene 10 — All the way to something buildable

**Screenshot:** `shots/06-06-architecture.png`
**On screen:** From "we should do this" to how it is built

**Narration:**
Selected use cases carry through into the design studio, where each becomes a build-ready brief: a reference architecture, drillable to control flow, a workflow mapped across n8n, Make, Airflow and code, a tool shortlist filtered to the platform the client actually runs, plus first drafts of governance, MLOps and runbooks.

The architecture layouts are authored per pattern — only the labels are generated — so the diagram cannot come out as nonsense. And where something was inferred rather than drawn from this client's diagnostic, the product says so on the section.

For most firms this is the extension of scope that pays for the platform. It is the difference between finishing at "you should do these six things" and finishing at something a client's engineering team can start on.

### Scene 11 — Built to run a firm, not a laptop

**Screenshot:** `shots/00-92-admin.png`
**On screen:** Multi-tenant, scoped access, server-side AI

**Narration:**
Some practical things, briefly, because they come up in procurement.

It is multi-tenant and cloud-hosted. Consultants can be scoped to named clients, enforced on the server, which is how you bring in a subcontractor or run two engagements that must not see each other. All AI calls run server-side and are metered per firm, so there are no API keys in anybody's browser. AI cost is reported per client, at cost, if your engagement letter passes it through. And there is a portfolio scorecard across every engagement your firm is running.

### Scene 12 — What it changes

**Screenshot:** `shots/04-02-synthesis-loaded.png`
**On screen:** Consistent · traceable · repeatable

**Narration:**
Three things, to close.

**Consistency.** Every executive gets the same rigour regardless of who was available that week, because the briefing rather than the interviewer sets the depth.

**Traceability.** Every number on the readout goes back to a person and a sentence, and you can show that in the room rather than promising to come back on it.

**Repeatability.** The same engagement runs the same way across your firm, which is what makes an AI readiness practice something you can staff rather than something that depends on the two people who have done it before.

The judgement stays with your consultants. What changes is that the assembly, the arithmetic and the audit trail no longer take up the week before the readout.
